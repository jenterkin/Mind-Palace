# How to get things done

You can have the best of the best for you, but they must be set up for success if you want to get anywhere. A department left to its' own devices will view the world through their own lens, as we all do. This inevitably leads to fragmentation on the prioritization of company goals, or even what those goals are. The misalignment leads to friction, which itself leads to adversity and silos when mixed a weak or negative culture.

What you're left with are fiefdoms working towards effectively different goals, fumbling at each step of an initiative because they're on different pages and not working together as a team.

A clear direction and structures that incentivize positive outcomes must be provided so that each department, team, and employee is aligned on what the business is trying to achieve, and are working together on the same goals, moving the company towards success.

There are countless ways to guide a company towards success, but each and every one of them must provide

- Tightly scoped and clearly defined goals. ^goals
- The right people in the right seats. ^people
- A (near) perfect understanding of what you're working on _and why_. ^knowledge
- Solid communication structures. ^communication
- A friendly, empathetic, and helpful culture. ^culture
- Focus. ^focus

## Setting goals

![[#^goals|^goals]]

### Focus on improving metrics

At the risk of stating the obvious: goals represent a metric you want to improve, ideally with a target and timeline.

- Increase GMV to $x/month by end of quarter.
- Introduce ARR offering, reach $y/year by end of quarter.
- Increase customer happiness by z% by end of quarter.

The goals' target and timeline are just as crucial as the goal itself -- without them, nothing meaningful is being conveyed. Of course we want to make the line go up and to the right, but what you do to accomplish a minor improvement slowly is very different than making a large impact quickly, and more often than not, you want to incentivize your departments, teams, and people to work on the more impactful, timely solution.

### Validate, validate, validate

### Make them achievable

It's always better to set a goal you're confident can be achieved than... well, not doing that. Reliably achieving goals gives you clairvoyance. Okay, more like a high degree of predictability, but that's still a super power! Predictability gives you the ability to plan (the better your predictability, the further into the future you can plan), understand which parts of the company need to grow and by how much, improve relations with investors and customers, etc. You also get cultural boosts that will pay dividends (a sense of security, confidence in the company and leadership) and avoid the pitfalls of the chaotic little-to-no-predictability approach: negative cultural impacts, worse public and investor relations, unrealistic incentive structures that lead to folks giving up on hitting their targets, to name a few.

To this end, goal setting _must_ be a conversation with those responsible with achieving the goals and feedback seriously considered. When all is said and done, stakeholders should be happy with the targets and confident it can be done. If you find that the two things are at ends, you need to figure out what can be done to increase confidence (e.g. hiring), or consider focusing on something else.

#### Acknowledge and accept risk

At the riskier risk of stating the even more obvious: Goals are just that, goals. You might achieve them, and you should do what you can to do so, but you might fail. In fact you certainly will given enough time, and that's expected, okay, and (spoiler for the next section) good. Anything can impact a timeline -- missing information when planning, market shifts, a key employee leaves the company, or maybe you were just wrong. Nobody can make guarantees, only estimates based on the information they have.

There are no certainties here, and taking a hardline pass/fail stance on achieving goals doesn't help anyone -- it's stressful to take the blame for something you had little-to-no control over and incentivizes those doing the work to take on safer, less impactful work instead. The important part is that we learn and improve going forward. When a goal slips, hold a retrospective and figure out what can be done better next time. When you achieve a goal, hold a retrospective and figure out what can be done better next time.

#### (Some) Risk and failure is good

Remember what I said about incentives in the previous section? For those of you skimming, I gochu:

> . . . taking a hardline pass/fail stance on achieving goals \[ . . . \] incentivizes those doing the work to take on safer, less impactful work instead.

Always, _always_ be aware of what behaviors you're incentivizing.

For the same reason we don't want to penalize people for letting a goal slip every once in a while, we actually want to _encourage_ a low failure rate -- this is how you can balance predictability with professional growth and taking on high risk/high reward projects (gambling). Encourage people to take chances by celebrating failure. Normalize it, learn from it, and present on it so we can all together.

And of course celebrate when those risks pay off! We did the hard thing and get to reap the rewards! 🎊
